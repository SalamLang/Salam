<h2 id="arith">Arithmetic</h2>
<p><code>+ - * / %</code> behave as they do in mathematics, and <code>**</code> raises to a power. Multiplication and division happen before addition and subtraction, and parentheses change the order.</p>
{{example.ops-arith}}
<p><code>+</code> also joins strings, and with a string on its left it turns a number into text: <code>"total: " + 5</code> is <code>"total: 5"</code>. The <a href="/learn/strings/">strings lesson</a> has more.</p>

<h2 id="divide">Division and remainder</h2>
<p>Dividing two whole numbers gives a whole number: <code>17 / 5</code> is 3, and <code>%</code> gives what is left over, 2. That pair is exactly what you need to split minutes into hours, to count how many tables a group needs, or to tell even numbers from odd ones.</p>
{{example.ops-divide}}
<p>Two details are worth remembering:</p>
<ul>
<li>Division cuts toward zero, so <code>-7 / 2</code> is -3, and the remainder takes the sign of the left side, so <code>-7 % 2</code> is -1. A test such as <code>n % 2 == 1</code> therefore misses negative odd numbers; <code>n % 2 != 0</code> catches them all.</li>
<li><code>%</code> works only on whole numbers; on decimals it is a compile error. Dividing by a literal <code>0</code> is a compile error too, but a zero that turns up at run time is not checked for you and can crash the program, so test the divisor first.</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="power">Powers</h2>
<p><code>**</code> raises a number to a power, and its result is always an <code>f64</code>, even for two whole numbers. That lets it handle <code>2 ** 0.5</code> and <code>2 ** -1</code>, but it means you convert with <code>as int</code> when you need a whole number back.</p>
{{example.ops-power}}
<p><code>**</code> groups from the right, so <code>2 ** 3 ** 2</code> is <code>2 ** 9</code>, and it binds tighter than a leading minus, so <code>-2 ** 2</code> is -4, as in mathematics. For the same reason <code>**=</code> works on an <code>f64</code> variable but not on an <code>int</code> one, because the result would not fit back in.</p>

<h2 id="compare">Comparison</h2>
<p>A comparison produces a <code>bool</code>. The operators are <code>==</code>, <code>!=</code>, <code>&lt;</code>, <code>&gt;</code>, <code>&lt;=</code> and <code>&gt;=</code>, and each has a word form as well: <code>eq</code>, <code>neq</code>, <code>lt</code>, <code>gt</code>, <code>lte</code> and <code>gte</code>. Join conditions with <code>and</code> and <code>or</code>, and invert one with <code>not</code>.</p>
{{example.ops-compare}}
<p>Strings compare with the same operators. <code>==</code> checks that the text is the same, and <code>&lt;</code> orders by character code, so every capital letter comes before every small one: <code>"Zebra" &lt; "apple"</code> is <code>true</code>.</p>
<p>Comparisons do not chain. <code>1 &lt; x &lt; 10</code> compares the <code>bool</code> from <code>1 &lt; x</code> with 10, and the compiler rejects it (cannot compare 'bool' and 'i32'); write <code>x &gt; 1 and x &lt; 10</code>.</p>

<h2 id="logic">Combining conditions</h2>
<p><code>and</code> and <code>or</code> stop as soon as the answer is known. If the left side of <code>and</code> is false, the right side never runs; if the left side of <code>or</code> is true, the same. That makes it safe to guard a risky check with a simple one, as the first <code>if</code> does here: the division only happens when <code>count</code> is not zero.</p>
{{example.ops-logic}}
<p>Only the third call to <code>check</code> ran. <code>not</code> binds tighter than any comparison, so <code>not count == 3</code> means <code>(not count) == 3</code> and is an error; write <code>not (count == 3)</code>, or simply <code>count != 3</code>. Salam has no <code>&amp;&amp;</code>, <code>||</code> or <code>!</code>; the compiler rejects them and names the word to write instead.</p>

<h2 id="ternary">The ternary operator</h2>
<p><code>condition ? a : b</code> gives <code>a</code> when the condition holds and <code>b</code> otherwise. Ternaries can be chained to pick from several ranges, and they read top to bottom: the first condition that holds wins.</p>
{{example.ops-ternary}}
<p>Both branches must have the same type: <code>n &gt; 3 ? "big" : 0</code> is an error. A chain of more than three or four cases is usually clearer as a <a href="/learn/match/">match</a>.</p>

<h2 id="compound">Changing a variable in place</h2>
<p><code>+=</code>, <code>-=</code>, <code>*=</code>, <code>/=</code> and <code>%=</code> apply an operator and store the result back in the variable, which must be <code>mut</code>. <code>+=</code> also appends to a string. <code>++</code> adds one and <code>--</code> takes one away.</p>
{{example.ops-compound}}
<p>Used as a value, <code>i++</code> gives the old value and then adds one, which is why <code>j</code> is 6 while <code>i</code> ends at 7; <code>++i</code> adds first. Code is easier to read when <code>++</code> and <code>--</code> stand on their own line.</p>

<h2 id="bitwise">Bitwise operators</h2>
<p><code>&amp;</code>, <code>|</code>, <code>^</code>, <code>~</code> and the shifts <code>&lt;&lt;</code> and <code>&gt;&gt;</code> work on the bits of a whole number.</p>
{{example.ops-bitwise}}
<p>Their everyday use is a set of yes or no options packed into one number, each option one bit. <code>|=</code> turns an option on, <code>&amp;= ~</code> turns it off and <code>&amp;</code> tests it. A shift right by 10 divides by 1024.</p>
{{example.ops-flags}}
<p>Keep the parentheses in <code>(perms &amp; WRITE) != 0</code>. As in C, <code>&amp;</code> binds more loosely than <code>==</code> and <code>!=</code>, so without them the comparison happens first. The compiler catches this one, since it leaves <code>&amp;</code> with a <code>bool</code> on one side. Bitwise operators accept whole numbers only.</p>

<h2 id="precedence">Which operator goes first</h2>
<p>From the tightest binding to the loosest. Operators on the same row are worked out from left to right, except <code>**</code>.</p>
