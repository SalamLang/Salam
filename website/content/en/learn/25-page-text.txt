<h2 id="shape">The shape of a page</h2>
<p>A <code>page:</code> block holds everything on the page. Before the first element you can set things that belong to the page itself: <code>title</code> is the text on the browser tab, <code>lang</code> and <code>dir</code> say which language and which direction the text has, and <code>background</code>, <code>color</code> and the other style properties at that level paint the whole page. Everything else is an element. An element opens with its name and a colon and closes with <code>end</code>, and what goes between is attributes and child elements, written as <code>name = value</code> with no commas.</p>
<p>The text of an element is its <code>content</code> attribute. It is always plain text: a <code>&lt;</code> or an <code>&amp;</code> in it is shown as it is and never becomes markup, so text that comes from a user can never break the page. Put several words in one string, or build a string with <code>+</code> from constants and numbers.</p>
{{example.web-text-article}}
<p>The preview above is the real page the compiler built. The <code>global</code> line is a style rule for the whole page, and you will meet it again in the CSS lessons; here it only gives the page a readable font and a comfortable width.</p>

<h2 id="headings">Headings</h2>
<p>Headings give a page its outline. <code>heading</code> takes a <code>size</code> from 1 to 6, and <code>h1</code> to <code>h6</code> are the same thing under their HTML names. Use a single level 1 for the title of the page and then go down one level at a time: a level 3 belongs under a level 2, not directly under a level 1. Browsers draw the levels in decreasing sizes, but choose the level for its meaning and change the size with CSS. When a heading has a subtitle, wrap both in a <code>heading group</code> as the example above does.</p>

<h2 id="paragraphs">Paragraphs, breaks and rules</h2>
<p><code>paragraph</code> is a block of text, and browsers leave space above and below it. Inside a paragraph, <code>break</code> starts a new line, which is right for an address or a poem and wrong as a way to add space, because empty space is a job for CSS. <code>line</code> draws a horizontal rule between two topics, and <code>word break</code> marks where a long word may be cut if it does not fit.</p>
<p>A paragraph can hold child elements next to its own <code>content</code>. The <code>content</code> text always comes first, and the children follow in the order you write them. So a sentence with a bold word in the middle is written as the first part in <code>content</code>, then the bold element, then a <code>span</code> with the rest. The <code>span</code> is a neutral container: it adds no meaning and no look of its own, which makes it the element for styling a few words.</p>

<h2 id="inline">Emphasis and inline text</h2>
<p>Inline elements live inside a line of text. Several of them look alike in the browser and differ in meaning, and the meaning is what screen readers and search engines use. <code>strong</code> is important text and <code>bold</code> is only bold type. <code>emphasis</code> is stress, the word you say louder, and <code>italic</code> is a different voice, such as a foreign word. <code>mark</code> highlights what is relevant right now, <code>strikethrough</code> shows what is no longer true, and <code>small</code> is for small print. <code>subscript</code> and <code>superscript</code> raise and lower text for formulas. <code>code</code>, <code>keyboard</code>, <code>sample</code> and <code>variable</code> are the four ways to write about computers.</p>
{{example.web-text-inline}}
<div class="note"><p>Pick the element for what the text <em>is</em>, not for how it looks. Looks change in CSS in one place, but the meaning stays with the text, and a reader that cannot see your page, such as a screen reader, depends on it.</p></div>

<h2 id="quotes">Quotations, code and preformatted text</h2>
<p><code>quote</code> is a short quotation inside a sentence and browsers add the quotation marks. <code>block quote</code> is a longer quotation set apart from the text. Both can take a <code>cite</code> attribute with the address of the source, and <code>cite</code> as an element names a work such as a book or a film. For a listing where every space and line break matters, use <code>preformatted</code>: it shows its text in a monospace font and keeps the layout exactly. Write a line break inside the string as <code>\n</code>.</p>
{{example.web-text-quote}}

<h2 id="edits">Edits, dates and abbreviations</h2>
<p><code>deleted</code> and <code>inserted</code> show what was taken out and what was put in, and both accept a <code>datetime</code> and a <code>cite</code>. <code>time</code> holds a date or a time in words for people and in a fixed format in its <code>datetime</code> for programs, and <code>data</code> does the same for any other value. An <code>abbreviation</code> keeps its full form in <code>title</code>, and <code>definition</code> marks the word that a sentence defines.</p>
{{example.web-text-edits}}

<h2 id="direction">Mixed directions and ruby</h2>
<p>A page in English often contains a Persian name, and a Persian page often contains an English one. Browsers decide the order of such text by a standard algorithm that usually works, but a name at the edge of a sentence can still pull the punctuation to the wrong side. Wrap a piece of text of unknown direction in <code>isolate</code> and it is laid out on its own, without affecting its neighbours. <code>direction override</code> is the blunt tool: it forces one direction whatever the characters say. <code>ruby</code> places a small annotation, usually a pronunciation, above the base text.</p>
{{example.web-text-direction}}

<h3 id="el-ruby"><code>ruby</code><span class="ref-alt"> (Persian: <code>آوانما</code>; HTML: <code>&lt;ruby&gt;</code>)</span></h3>
<p>Annotation for East Asian and other scripts, a small pronunciation guide shown above or beside the base text. It holds the base text plus <code>ruby text</code> elements.</p>
<pre class="code snip" dir="ltr"><code>ruby:
    content = &quot;漢&quot;
    ruby text: content = &quot;han&quot; end
end</code></pre>
<h3 id="el-ruby-text"><code>ruby text</code><span class="ref-alt"> (Persian: <code>متن آوانما</code>; HTML: <code>&lt;rt&gt;</code>)</span></h3>
<p>The small annotation text of a <code>ruby</code>.</p>
<pre class="code snip" dir="ltr"><code>ruby:
    content = &quot;東京&quot;
    ruby text: content = &quot;Tōkyō&quot; end
end</code></pre>
<p class="ref-meta">Only inside: <code>ruby</code></p>
<h3 id="el-ruby-parenthesis"><code>ruby parenthesis</code><span class="ref-alt"> (Persian: <code>پرانتز آوانما</code>; HTML: <code>&lt;rp&gt;</code>)</span></h3>
<p>Parentheses shown only by browsers that cannot draw ruby, so the annotation still reads as <code>漢(han)</code>.</p>
<pre class="code snip" dir="ltr"><code>ruby:
    content = &quot;漢&quot;
    ruby parenthesis: content = &quot;(&quot; end
    ruby text: content = &quot;han&quot; end
    ruby parenthesis: content = &quot;)&quot; end
end</code></pre>
<p class="ref-meta">Only inside: <code>ruby</code></p>
<h3 id="el-isolate"><code>isolate</code><span class="ref-alt"> (Persian: <code>جداساز جهت</code>; HTML: <code>&lt;bdi&gt;</code>)</span></h3>
<p>Isolates a piece of text from the direction of the text around it. It is the safe way to put a name of unknown direction, such as a user name, inside a sentence.</p>
<pre class="code snip" dir="ltr"><code>paragraph:
    content = &quot;User &quot;
    isolate: content = &quot;محمد&quot; end
    span: content = &quot; left a comment.&quot; end
end</code></pre>
<h3 id="el-direction-override"><code>direction override</code><span class="ref-alt"> (Persian: <code>جهت اجباری</code>; HTML: <code>&lt;bdo&gt;</code>)</span></h3>
<p>Forces the direction of its text, ignoring what the characters say. <code>dir</code> is required.</p>
<pre class="code snip" dir="ltr"><code>direction override: dir = &quot;rtl&quot; content = &quot;abc 123&quot; end</code></pre>
<p class="ref-meta">Required: <code>dir</code></p>
