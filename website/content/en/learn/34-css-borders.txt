<h2 id="border">The border shorthand</h2>
<p>A border is a line drawn between the padding of a box and its margin. It has three parts: a <em>width</em>, a <em>style</em> and a <em>color</em>. The shorthand <code>border</code> takes all three in one string, in any order: <code>"2px solid #0f766e"</code>. A bare number written without quotes, such as <code>6</code>, means pixels. The style decides what is drawn: <code>solid</code> is a plain line, <code>dashed</code> and <code>dotted</code> are broken lines, <code>double</code> is two thin lines with a gap, and <code>groove</code>, <code>ridge</code>, <code>inset</code> and <code>outset</code> imitate a carved or raised edge by shading two sides lighter and two darker. <code>none</code> draws nothing and <code>hidden</code> does the same, but wins when two borders meet in a table.</p>
{{example.web-border-styles}}
<p>The example uses a small component, <code>Sample</code>, so each border style is one line. It passes the style name to <code>border style</code> and also shows it as text. The same three parts exist as separate properties: <code>border width</code>, <code>border style</code> and <code>border color</code>.</p>
<div class="note"><p>The style is the part people forget. The default style is <code>none</code>, so <code>border color = "red"</code> or <code>border width = 5</code> on their own draw nothing at all. Always write a style, either in the shorthand or with <code>border style = solid</code>.</p></div>
<p>The three longhands take from one to four values, and the rule is the same for each of them. One value applies to all four sides. Two values are the top and bottom, then the left and right. Three values are the top, then the left and right, then the bottom. Four values go clockwise from the top: top, right, bottom, left. Padding and margin use the same rule, so you only learn it once.</p>
<p>A border is part of the box. With the default sizing a box of <code>width = 200</code> and a border of 6px on each side becomes 212px wide. Many pages add a global rule <code>box sizing = "border-box"</code>, which makes the width include padding and border, and the next lessons assume you know both ways.</p>

<h2 id="sides">One side at a time</h2>
<p>Each side has its own shorthand and its own three longhands. <code>border top</code>, <code>border right</code>, <code>border bottom</code> and <code>border left</code> take the usual width, style and color. <code>border top color</code> and its twelve relatives change one part of one side. The example shows the most common uses: a thick stripe on the left of a warning, an underline under a heading, and two ways to give the four sides different widths and colors in a single line.</p>
{{example.web-border-sides}}
<p>When a side has no border you can leave it out. The usual way to build a mixed border is to set <code>border</code> first and then overwrite the one side that must differ, for example <code>border = "1px solid #cbd5e1"</code> followed by <code>border bottom color = "#0f766e"</code>. The later property wins, so the order of the lines matters.</p>

<h2 id="logical">Logical sides for any language</h2>
<p><code>left</code> and <code>right</code> are wrong for a page that is read from right to left: a stripe at the left of an English quotation belongs at the right of a Persian one. The logical properties name the sides by the direction of the text instead. The <em>inline</em> direction is the way a line of text runs. <em>Inline start</em> is where a line begins, which is the left in English and the right in Persian or Arabic, and <em>inline end</em> is the opposite side. The <em>block</em> direction is the way lines stack, so <em>block start</em> is the top and <em>block end</em> is the bottom in ordinary horizontal text.</p>
<p>That gives <code>border inline start</code>, <code>border inline end</code>, <code>border block start</code> and <code>border block end</code>, each with its width, style and color. The shorthand <code>border inline</code> sets both inline sides and <code>border block</code> sets both block sides, and their color, style and width forms take one or two values (start, then end). In the example the same line of code puts the stripe on the left of the English box and on the right of the Persian box, because the second box has <code>dir = "rtl"</code>.</p>
{{example.web-border-logical}}
<p>Prefer the logical names in anything that may be shown in more than one language. In a Persian-only project the physical names are fine, and they are often what you want for something that is tied to the screen, such as a shadow.</p>

<h2 id="radius">Rounded corners</h2>
<p><code>border radius</code> rounds the corners. A single length makes circular corners of that radius. Two to four values follow the corner order: with two, the first is for the top left and bottom right corners and the second for the top right and bottom left; with four they go clockwise from the top left. A slash makes oval corners: <code>50% / 30%</code> sets a horizontal radius of 50% and a vertical radius of 30%. A radius larger than the box can fit is reduced, so <code>999px</code> is the usual way to make a pill, and <code>50%</code> on a square makes a circle.</p>
<p>Each corner also has its own property: <code>border top left radius</code>, <code>border top right radius</code>, <code>border bottom left radius</code> and <code>border bottom right radius</code>, and the logical versions <code>border start start radius</code>, <code>border start end radius</code>, <code>border end start radius</code> and <code>border end end radius</code>. The first word after <code>border</code> is the block side and the second is the inline side, so <code>border start end radius</code> is the top right corner in English and the top left in Persian.</p>
{{example.web-border-radius}}
<p>Rounding shapes the background, the border and the shadow, but it does not clip the content. A child that is larger than the box still sticks out of its rounded corner unless the box has <code>overflow = hidden</code>.</p>

<h2 id="image">Border images</h2>
<p>A border does not have to be a line. <code>border image source</code> draws a picture or a gradient in the border area instead. The picture is cut into nine pieces by <code>border image slice</code>: four corners, four edges and a middle. The corners go into the corners of the border as they are, the edges are stretched or repeated along the sides, and the middle is thrown away unless you add <code>fill</code>. This is called a nine-slice frame, and it lets one small picture fit boxes of any size.</p>
<p>The properties that control it are <code>border image slice</code> (how far from each edge of the picture to cut, as a number of the picture's own pixels or a percentage), <code>border image width</code> (how thick the frame is drawn, a length or a multiple of the border width), <code>border image outset</code> (how far it reaches outside the box) and <code>border image repeat</code> (<code>stretch</code>, <code>repeat</code>, <code>round</code> or <code>space</code>). <code>border image</code> is the shorthand for all five, in the order <code>source slice / width / outset repeat</code>. The border itself must still have a width, and a transparent color is the usual choice so that nothing shows through under the picture.</p>
{{example.web-border-image}}
<p>The frame in the example is a small SVG picture written as a data URL, with an amber circle in each corner. Because the corners are copied and never stretched, the circles stay round on a box of any size, and <code>round</code> repeats the edges a whole number of times so no piece is cut. A gradient works as a source too, and with a slice of 1 it makes the popular gradient border: <code>border image = "linear-gradient(135deg, #f59e0b, #db2777) 1"</code>. The one thing a border image cannot do is follow <code>border radius</code>, because the image replaces the line that the radius would have curved.</p>

<h2 id="outline">Outlines and focus rings</h2>
<p><code>outline</code> looks like a border and takes the same width, style and color, but it is different in three ways. It is drawn outside the box and takes no room in the layout, so adding one never moves anything. It can be pushed away from the box with <code>outline offset</code>, or pulled inside with a negative offset. And it is the tool of the browser for showing keyboard focus. The longhands are <code>outline color</code>, <code>outline style</code> (the border styles and also <code>auto</code>, the browser's own ring) and <code>outline width</code>.</p>
<p>Focus is the element that receives the keyboard. A person who cannot use a mouse presses <kbd>Tab</kbd> to move from link to button to field, and the focus ring is the only thing that shows where they are. Write the state as a prefix: <code>focus outline = "3px solid #2563eb"</code> applies only while the element has focus. Click into the example and press <kbd>Tab</kbd>: the button gets a blue ring set three pixels away, and the input gets a pink one.</p>
{{example.web-border-outline}}
<div class="note"><p>Never write <code>outline = none</code> to get rid of the ring that browsers draw around a focused control, unless you replace it with another clearly visible focus style. Without a ring, a keyboard user cannot tell where they are, and the page fails the accessibility rules that many countries require. The right fix for an ugly ring is a better looking ring, not no ring. The ring should have a contrast ratio of at least 3 to 1 against what is around it.</p></div>

<h2 id="shadow">Box shadows</h2>
<p><code>box shadow</code> draws a shadow behind the box, shaped like its rounded corners. One shadow is written as up to five parts: <code>x y blur spread color</code>. The first two numbers move the shadow right and down (negative numbers move it left and up). <em>Blur</em> softens the edge: 0 is sharp and a bigger number is softer. <em>Spread</em> makes the shadow bigger or, if negative, smaller than the box. The color is almost always a transparent black such as <code>rgb(0 0 0 / 20%)</code>, because a shadow lets the page show through. Put <code>inset</code> first and the shadow is drawn inside the box, as if it were pressed in.</p>
<p>Separate several shadows with commas and the first one is on top. A pair of shadows, one close and tight and one far and soft, looks far more natural than a single one, which is how the "layered" card gets its depth. A shadow with no blur and a spread of 4px is a ring that follows the corners, and a blurry shadow with a bright color is a glow.</p>
{{example.web-border-shadow}}
<p>The next example lets you try the values. The sliders write a new <code>box-shadow</code> and <code>border-radius</code> to the card and print the CSS they made, so you can read how each number changes the look.</p>
{{example.web-border-play}}
<div class="note"><p>A shadow is only decoration and never changes the layout, so it can overlap the neighbours and it can be cut by a parent with <code>overflow = hidden</code>. For text use <code>text shadow</code>, and for the shape of an image with transparent parts use the <code>drop-shadow()</code> filter from the previous lesson.</p></div>

<h2 id="special">Broken boxes and new corner shapes</h2>
<p>An inline element such as a <code>span</code> can wrap onto several lines, and then its padding, border and background have to be drawn on more than one piece. <code>box decoration break</code> chooses how. With <code>slice</code>, the default, the element is drawn as if it were one long box that has been cut with scissors, so the left edge appears only on the first piece and the right edge only on the last. With <code>clone</code> every piece is a complete box with its own edges, padding and corners. Use <code>clone</code> for highlights and tags that may wrap.</p>
{{example.web-border-break}}
<p>The <code>corner shape</code> properties change what a rounded corner looks like. Until now the curve was always a quarter circle. <code>corner shape</code> can make it a <code>squircle</code> (a softer, superelliptical curve, like app icons), a <code>bevel</code> (cut straight across), a <code>scoop</code> (curved inward) or a <code>notch</code> (a square cut inward), and each corner has its own property, such as <code>corner top left shape</code>. The shape is applied to the corners that <code>border radius</code> sets, so the radius is still needed to say how large the corner is.</p>
{{example.web-border-corners}}
<div class="note"><p>Corner shapes are new and so far only Chromium browsers draw them. In other browsers the corners simply stay round, which is a fine fallback because the box still works. Do not make a design depend on them yet. Borders in tables, with <code>border collapse</code>, are covered in <a href="/learn/css-more/">the lesson on more properties</a>.</p></div>

<h2 id="checking">How the compiler checks values</h2>
<p>Every style value in a page is checked while the page is compiled, not in the browser. A browser silently ignores a CSS value it does not understand, and the page just looks wrong with no message. In Salam a bad value stops the build with error <code>E018</code>. The message names the property, shows the value you wrote and says what was expected. For example, writing a style of <code>wobbly</code> in <code>border style</code> reports <code>invalid value for 'border style': 'wobbly' is not 'none', 'hidden', 'dotted', ...</code> followed by the file, line and column where the value is. A radius of <code>big</code> reports that it is not a length or a percentage, and a <code>border</code> shorthand with parts that do not belong together says that it is not a valid combination for this property.</p>
<p>The check works on the shorthands as well. It splits <code>border</code> into its parts and checks each part against its own rules, so a typo in a color name inside <code>"2px solid reed"</code> is found too. Persian files accept the Persian value names such as <code>یکپارچه</code> and <code>خط‌چین</code> in the same places, and the messages are in Persian. The tables below show the values each property accepts.</p>
