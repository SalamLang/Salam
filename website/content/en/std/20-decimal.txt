pkg.intro <<
<p>In <code>f64</code>, 0.1 + 0.2 is not exactly 0.3. For science and graphics that tiny error does not matter; for money it does, because a lost cent in the accounts is not acceptable. The <code>decimal</code> package keeps a number as a <a href="/std/bigint/">bigint</a> and a <em>scale</em>, the count of digits after the point, so 10.05 is 1005 with scale 2 and no error can creep in. Use it for prices, invoices, balances, tax and interest. Use <code>f64</code> and <a href="/std/math/">math</a> for measurements and statistics, and plain <code>i64</code> cents when every amount has the same two places and never exceeds about 92 quadrillion.</p>
<p>Addition, subtraction and multiplication are always exact. The scale of a sum is the larger of the two, and the scale of a product is the two added together. Division may not terminate, as one third does not, so <code>Div</code> takes the number of places you want and a <code>Rounding</code> method. Seven methods are offered; accounting usually wants <code>HalfEven</code>, and customer-facing totals often <code>HalfUp</code>.</p>
<p>What people get wrong:</p>
<ul>
<li>Letting the scale grow. Each <code>Mul</code> adds the scales together, so a balance multiplied by a rate every month carries more digits each time. Round to cents after every step that a statement shows.</li>
<li>Expecting school rounding from the formatters. <code>ToStrFixed</code>, <code>FormatGrouped</code>, <code>Round</code> and <code>Percent</code> round half to even, so 2.345 shows as 2.34. Call <code>RoundHalfUp</code> first when you want 2.35.</li>
<li>Dividing by zero. <code>Div</code>, <code>DivRound</code> and <code>DivInt</code> return 0 instead of stopping the program, so check the divisor yourself.</li>
<li>Copying with <code>=</code>. A <code>Dec</code> holds a <code>bigint.Int</code>, so <code>b := a</code> shares its digits; use <code>Clone</code> for an independent copy, and free each number once.</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="guide-float">Why not f64?</h2>
<p>Adding 0.1 ten times in <code>f64</code> gives 0.9999999999999999, because 0.1 has no exact binary form. The same loop with <code>Dec</code> gives exactly 1.0. Build amounts from text with <code>Parse</code> or from whole cents with <code>FromScaled</code>; <code>FromFloat</code> works when you already have an <code>f64</code>, as long as you say how many places it should keep.</p>
{{example.decimal-guide-float}}

<h2 id="guide-invoice">An invoice with discount and tax</h2>
<p>Prices usually come from a database or a form as text, so <code>Parse</code> is the natural start. <code>MulInt</code> multiplies by a quantity without any rounding, and <code>Percent</code> takes a percentage and rounds the result to the places you ask for, half to even. Print with <code>ToStrFixed</code> so that 12 shows as 12.00, or with <code>FormatGrouped</code> for thousands separators.</p>
{{example.decimal-guide-invoice}}
<p>Round once per step that a real invoice shows (the discount, the tax), not at the very end only. Customers and tax offices check each printed line, so each line must already be in cents. The example does not free its numbers to stay short; a long-running server should <code>Free</code> each one, as the split example does.</p>

<h2 id="guide-interest">Interest and the cost of rounding</h2>
<p>A bank pays interest in whole cents every year, and those rounded cents then earn interest themselves. The example runs five years with yearly rounding and compares the result with the exact formula: the difference is one cent. <code>Div</code> and <code>DivInt</code> always ask for the number of places and a <code>Rounding</code> method, because a division such as one twelfth has no end.</p>
{{example.decimal-guide-interest}}

<h2 id="guide-split">Splitting a bill without losing a cent</h2>
<p>Dividing 100.00 by 3 and rounding gives 33.33 three times, which adds up to 99.99. <code>Split</code> gives the missing cent to the first share, so the shares always add up to the original amount. <code>Allocate</code> does the same for shares in a ratio, such as profit split between partners.</p>
{{example.decimal-guide-split}}
<p>The leftover smallest units go to the first shares, one each, in both functions; <code>Allocate</code> does not look at which share lost the most to rounding. <code>Split</code> with fewer than one share, and <code>Allocate</code> with no ratios, a negative ratio or ratios that add up to zero, return an empty vector. Both return a new vector of numbers; free it with <code>FreeAll</code>, which releases every number and the vector.</p>

<h2 id="guide-parse">What Parse accepts</h2>
<p><code>Parse</code> skips spaces around the number, accepts a sign, Persian digits, underscores between digits and scientific notation. It refuses a comma as the decimal separator and the Persian separator <code>٫</code>: replace either with a dot before parsing if your users type them. <code>ParseOr</code> returns zero on failure, which suits optional fields but hides typing mistakes.</p>
{{example.decimal-guide-parse}}
<p>Scientific notation can lower the scale: <code>2.5e3</code> becomes 2500 with scale 0, and <code>1.50e1</code> becomes 15.0.</p>

<h2 id="guide-pitfalls">Rounding, growing scales and zero</h2>
<p>The three surprises from the list above, in one program. The price is a typical half-cent case, the repeated <code>Mul</code> is a monthly rate applied four times, and the zero divisor is a team with no members.</p>
{{example.decimal-guide-pitfalls}}
<p><code>FromFloat</code> has a limit of its own: it multiplies the <code>f64</code> by ten for each place and converts to <code>i64</code>, so a value times 10<sup>scale</sup> must stay below about 9.2 × 10<sup>18</sup>. For large amounts, parse text instead.</p>
